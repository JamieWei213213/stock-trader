# Research memo: what the evidence says about our architecture (2026-09-03)

Three questions were researched in parallel: what LLM trading-agent systems actually achieve, how a short-horizon stock screener should be built, and what free data could give our research agents more than headlines. Sources are linked at the end of each section. The short version: our overall shape (cheap LLM upstream, deterministic risk code downstream) is the shape the best evidence supports, but two specific pieces of our design are wrong and fixable, and the honest ceiling on this project is lower than it feels.

## 1. LLM trading agents: real but small, and mostly oversold

The famous multi-agent frameworks (TradingAgents, FinMem, FinRobot) report eye-catching returns, but the evidence underneath is thin. TradingAgents' headline result (26.6% on AAPL vs a losing buy-and-hold) is a three-month backtest on three tickers, and the authors themselves say the Sharpe ratio "exceeded our expected empirical range." A 2026 replication ("The Alpha Illusion") added commissions, spread, and token costs and cut its Sharpe from 0.43 to 0.22, below buy-and-hold. FinMem's returns fall roughly 72% when evaluated outside the model's training window, which suggests it was partly remembering the future. A March 2026 survey of 77 agentic-trading studies found only two with time-consistent splits, one that reported transaction costs, and none fully reproducible.

The cleanest study of whether an LLM reading news predicts returns is Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023, now in the Journal of Financial Economics). GPT-4 scoring headlines for 4,000 US stocks produced about 34 basis points per day long-short before costs, with a 58% hit rate. That is a real signal. But it is next-day only, it is concentrated in small caps and in negative news, it halves at 5 bps round-trip costs and disappears at 20 bps, and its Sharpe decayed from 6.5 in late 2021 to 1.2 by 2024 as everyone started doing it. For a retail account trading large caps with a 1–10 day hold, the expected edge from headline reading is close to zero after costs.

Three design findings matter for us. First, multi-agent debate (bull agent vs bear agent, reflection loops) has no trading-specific evidence of beating one well-prompted agent; a commodity-ETF study found a debate agent's Sharpe difference was −0.004 with p = 0.77. It mostly costs tokens. We should not add it. Second, LLMs systematically over-extrapolate the most recent week's return, about ten times more than two weeks prior, and are optimistic by default; prompting only partly fixes this. Third, Glasserman & Lin found that anonymizing company names in headlines before scoring improved out-of-sample performance, because the model's background knowledge of famous firms pollutes its read of the actual news. That is a cheap change we can make.

The strongest consensus recommendation across all the audits is exactly what we already do: keep the LLM upstream as an information reader, have it emit a structured signal, and let non-LLM code decide position sizes and enforce risk. StockBench (14 models on 20 DJIA stocks, 2025) found the main real benefit of LLM agents was lower drawdown, not higher return, and that performance degraded as the stock count grew from 5 to 30.

## 2. Our screener is pointed the wrong way

This is the most useful finding. Momentum, the effect with thirty years of evidence behind it, is a 3-to-12-month phenomenon. The standard construction ranks stocks on months t-12 through t-2 and deliberately skips the most recent month, because at the one-month horizon the literature finds the opposite: short-term reversal, where last week's and last month's winners underperform. Our "momentum" variant ranks on 20-day return, which is precisely the window where reversal dominates. On a large-cap universe the two effects partly cancel, which is consistent with the null result we got. In other words, we did not test momentum; we tested a signal the literature expects to be slightly negative.

For a 1–10 day hold on liquid US stocks, the only effect with robust net-of-cost evidence is short-term reversal: buying recent losers. Restricted to large caps it earns roughly 30–50 bps per week long-short, less long-only, and it has weakened over the last twenty years. Industry- or residual-adjusted versions (buying stocks that fell relative to their sector, not stocks whose whole sector fell) are stronger and trade less. Our "pullback" variant is the closest thing we have to this, and the best version combines both ideas: buy short-term losers within long-term winners, i.e. a 5-day pullback in a name with strong 12-2 momentum.

Other candidate effects checked out worse. Post-earnings-announcement drift, historically the best-known anomaly, has been dead since about 2006 for anything above micro-cap; the price reaction now happens on day zero. The high-volume return premium is real but about 0.5% over a month in large caps, marginal after costs. The overnight-vs-intraday split is striking (momentum returns accrue almost entirely overnight) but is not tradeable as a standalone; its practical lesson is to enter swing positions near the close rather than at the open, which is the opposite of our 6:05am market-on-open orders.

On backtest hygiene: with 25 names over four years our null result was uninformative, not a rejection. A daily strategy with a true Sharpe of 1.0 needs about three years of data to be distinguishable from luck at 95% confidence; a Sharpe of 0.5 needs about twelve. Harvey, Liu & Zhu argue that after accounting for how many signals researchers have tried, a new one needs t > 3, not 2, and every variant we sweep counts as a trial. Our universe is also today's 500 most liquid names, which is survivorship bias; a proper test defines the universe as of each rebalance date.

## 3. Risk rules: ours are fixed percentages, they should scale with volatility

Practitioner consensus (not academic evidence, but consistent) is to set stops at 1.5–3× the 14-day ATR below entry rather than a fixed 2% or 4%, and to size positions so that a stop-out loses a fixed fraction of equity, typically 0.5–1%: shares = (equity × risk%) / (ATR × stop multiple). Total open risk across all positions ("portfolio heat") is kept under about 6%. Time stops matter too, because reversal returns decay within days.

Our current $100k rules (2% stop, 4% target, 15% cap) mean a stock like HOOD with a 4% daily range will get stopped by noise most of the time, while a sleepy utility with a 1% range never reaches its target. None of this creates an edge; it just stops a small edge from being eaten by drawdowns.

Sources: [ATR stops](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-atr-stop-loss-strategies-for-risk-control/) · [Position sizing, Tharp](https://www.theoptionpremium.com/p/position-sizing-van-tharp-golden-rule) · [Portfolio heat](https://www.liberatedstocktrader.com/portfolio-heat-calculator/)

## 4. Free data we are not using yet

Ranked by value per hour of work. Alpaca's own extras first, because they need no new key: the news endpoint accepts `include_content=true` and returns full article bodies (far better LLM context than a headline), and there are free screener endpoints for top movers and most-actives that could feed candidate generation. Alpaca's corporate-actions feed covers splits and dividends but not earnings dates.

SEC EDGAR is free, keyless (just a User-Agent header, 10 requests per second), and gives full-text search over 8-K filings ("why did this stock move"), XBRL company facts for a two-line fundamentals summary, and Form 4 insider transactions; the `edgartools` Python package wraps all of it.

Finnhub's free tier (60 calls per minute, one key) is the one third-party signup worth doing: its earnings calendar is the simplest way to stop the bot buying the day before a report, and it adds analyst recommendation counts and insider sentiment. FRED gives daily VIX and rates for a market-regime flag. Options flow has no good free source; skip it. Yahoo/yfinance is unreliable in 2026 and should not be in the bot's hot path.

Sources: [Alpaca news API](https://alpaca.markets/blog/introducing-news-api-for-real-time-fiancial-news/) · [Alpaca movers screener](https://docs.alpaca.markets/reference/movers-1) · [SEC developer resources](https://www.sec.gov/about/developer-resources) · [edgartools](https://github.com/dgunning/edgartools) · [Finnhub pricing](https://finnhub.io/pricing) · [FRED API](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/docs/api/fred/v2/api_key.html)

## 5. What I recommend changing, in order

1. **Fix the screener.** Replace the 20-day "momentum" default with a reversal-in-uptrend rule: rank on 5-day pullback within names whose 12-2 month return is in the top half, sector-adjusted if we can. Add true 12-2 momentum as its own variant so the sweep tests the real thing. Make the backtest define the universe by trailing dollar volume as of each date to reduce survivorship bias. Rerun the sweep on 500 names and require t > 3 before believing anything.
2. **Volatility-scaled risk.** Stops at 2× ATR, targets at 2× the stop distance, size so a stop-out costs 0.75% of equity, portfolio heat cap 6%, time stop at max_hold_days. This replaces the fixed 2%/4% and 4%/8% rules.
3. **Earnings blackout.** Finnhub calendar; never open a position within 2 trading days of a report.
4. **Better prompt inputs.** Anonymize company names in what Haiku reads, pass article bodies not just headlines, add an explicit instruction against over-weighting the last week's move, and have Haiku emit an expected 5-day return and confidence rather than "conviction 1–5" so the manager can compare across stocks numerically.
5. **Entry timing.** Consider moving the daily entry to a 12:45pm cycle (orders fill near the close) instead of 6:05am, since the evidence says swing returns accrue overnight. Worth testing in the backtest first.
6. **Do not add** debate agents, more analyst personas, or hourly cycles. The evidence says they add cost, not edge.

## 6. Honest expectations

The best-documented signals available to us are worth tens of basis points per week gross, and the LLM overlay adds a small, decaying, next-day effect that is strongest in small caps we mostly can't afford in the $1k account. After spread and slippage, a realistic outcome for a well-built version of this system on large caps is roughly break-even to slightly positive, with lower drawdown than buy-and-hold. That is a respectable result for a $4 server and $10 of API credit, and the month of paper trading will mostly teach us about failure modes rather than confirm an edge. Anyone promising more than that from headline-reading agents is, per the replication studies, not accounting for costs.
